Science

A More Complete Picture of Tissue Biology

We read protein biology, spatial context and disease state together, because the relationships between them are what decide targets, biomarkers and therapeutic strategy.

The problem

Most ways of reading tissue give something up.

Every established method answers part of the question well, and discards another part along the way.

Averaging

Bulk measurements report many cells at once. A small population behaving differently disappears into the mean.

Dissociation

Breaking tissue into single cells recovers detail about each one, but gives up where it sat and what it sat beside.

Panels answer their own question

A panel reports on the markers it was designed around. What was left off it stays invisible, however many channels it carries.

What we connect

Three readings of the same tissue.

01

Protein biology

Which proteins are present and in what amount — the molecules that carry out the work, rather than a proxy for it.

02

Spatial context

Where those signals sit, and which cells are neighbors. Tumor, immune and stromal cells influence one another continuously.

03

Disease state

Not only which cells are present, but the state they are in and how they respond.

How we work

More from the same tissue.

Static staining is static

Antibody staining reports one fixed moment and stops there. The same tissue holds far more than a static image can carry.

One slice is not the sample

A single section samples a fraction of the material in front of you. We extend sampling well beyond that plane.

The signals that get lost first

Rare cells and low-abundance proteins disappear before anything else. We make them visible, and show how they respond.

The opportunity

Tissue is the most underused asset in oncology R&D.

The answer was never more markers. Yarra Biosystems is developing a platform that asks a different question of the same tissue.

Why Yarra Biosystems?

Named for where the journey began.

Yarra Biosystems takes its name from the Yarra River, or Birrarung, in Melbourne, Australia, where our cofounder Savanh Chantha-Phavong’s scientific journey began.

The Birrarung rises in the forested ranges of Victoria, gathering tributaries as it moves through different landscapes and communities before winding through Melbourne and opening into Port Phillip Bay. For the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung people, the Traditional Owners of much of the land along the Birrarung, the river has long held cultural, ecological and community significance.

That idea of convergence reflects how Yarra Biosystems is being built.

Biology does not happen in isolation. A cell’s behavior is shaped by the proteins it expresses, the signals it receives, the cells around it, and the physical environment of the tissue itself. Tumor, immune and stromal cells continuously influence one another, while proteins translate those interactions into the biological states we observe.

Understanding disease therefore requires more than looking at any one cell, marker or technology alone.

Yarra Biosystems brings together pathology, oncology, spatial biology, engineering, biomaterials and molecular technologies. Just as importantly, we bring together people, ideas, expertise and different ways of thinking.

Like tributaries joining a river, each contributes something different. Together, they create the opportunity to see biology more completely.

Our ambition is to use that convergence to unlock far more information and insight from tissue than has previously been possible, helping uncover new therapeutic targets, clinically meaningful biomarkers, and ultimately better therapies.

Our work now spans continents. Our cofounder is based in the San Francisco Bay Area, while our name remains rooted in Melbourne and the place where it began.

The Birrarung begins in the ranges, gathers strength from what joins it along the way, crosses boundaries and opens toward a much wider horizon.

That is what Yarra Biosystems represents: bringing together the right people, ideas, expertise and technologies to build something capable of revealing far more from tissue than any one approach could alone.
